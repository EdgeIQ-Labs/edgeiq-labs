Third-Party Security Verification: What to Actually Check Before Signing

You've received the vendor's SOC 2 report and their completed security questionnaire. Everything looks clean. But compliance documentation tells you what a vendor claims about their security posture, not what that posture actually is. Here's how to verify.

The Problem with Paper-Based Verification

Most third-party security verification processes are document reviews. You request a SOC 2 Type II report, review the auditor's opinion letter, check for exceptions in the testing results, and file it away. Or you send a 200-question SIG Lite questionnaire and accept the vendor's self-reported answers at face value.

This approach has two fundamental weaknesses. First, compliance reports are point-in-time assessments covering a specific audit period. A vendor can be fully compliant during their audit window and introduce significant security gaps three weeks later when they deploy an untested update or hire a contractor with excessive privileges. Second, questionnaires measure a vendor's ability to answer security questions correctly, not their actual implementation of those controls.

Effective third-party security verification requires combining documentation review with independent technical validation. You need to see what the vendor's infrastructure actually exposes to the internet, not just what their compliance team says it should expose.

Beyond the SOC 2 Report: What to Look For

SOC 2 reports are valuable but insufficient on their own. When reviewing them, pay attention to these often-overlooked sections:

Audit Scope and System Description

The system description defines what was actually audited. Vendors frequently scope audits narrowly to cover only their flagship product while excluding newer services, acquired platforms, or internal tools that still process customer data. If the service you're buying isn't explicitly named in the system description, the audit doesn't cover it. Request clarification on any ambiguity before proceeding.

Exceptions and Management Responses

Auditors test controls and document exceptions where controls failed or weren't implemented as designed. Read every exception carefully. Pay particular attention to management's response — vague commitments to "address this in the next quarter" without specific remediation plans suggest the issue may persist. Multiple exceptions in related control areas indicate systemic problems rather than isolated lapses.

Subservice Organizations

If your vendor relies on subservice organizations (cloud providers, payment processors, data centers), the report should specify which ones and whether carve-out or inclusive methods were used. Carve-out means the subservice wasn't audited as part of this report. You may need separate verification for critical subservices that handle your data.

Technical Validation: Independent Verification Checks

Documentation review should be paired with technical checks you can perform independently. These don't require special access or vendor cooperation — they examine what's publicly observable.

Core Technical Verification Checklist

  • SSL/TLS Configuration: Valid certificate? Strong cipher suites? TLS 1.2+ enforced? Certificate transparency logs show issuance history and can reveal unexpected certificates issued for the vendor's domains.
  • Email Authentication: SPF records present and properly configured? DKIM signing enabled? DMARC policy at p=quarantine or p=reject? Vendors without email authentication controls are spoofable, creating phishing risk for your organization through impersonation.
  • Exposed Services: Port scans of public IP ranges reveal admin panels, development servers, database ports, or legacy services that shouldn't be internet-accessible. Each exposed service is a potential attack vector.
  • DNS Records: MX records point to legitimate mail infrastructure? CNAME records reference expected services? Unexpected DNS entries can indicate compromised accounts or shadow IT deployments.
  • Security Headers: Content-Security-Policy, X-Content-Type-Options, Strict-Transport-Security headers present and properly configured? Missing headers suggest incomplete defense-in-depth implementation.
  • Breach History: Has the vendor appeared in known breach databases? Have employee credentials from their domain been exposed? Past incidents aren't disqualifying, but response quality and transparency matter significantly.
  • Domain Age and Registration: Recently registered domains, privacy-protected WHOIS records, or frequent registrar changes can signal instability or intentional obfuscation. Established vendors typically have stable registration histories.

Reading Between the Lines of Security Questionnaires

When vendors complete security questionnaires, certain response patterns warrant closer examination:

Red Flag: Every answer is "Yes" with no qualifications or exceptions. Real security programs have trade-offs, compensating controls, and work-in-progress items. Perfect responses suggest either exceptional maturity or insufficient honesty.

Red Flag: Answers reference policies without evidence of implementation. "We have an incident response plan" differs materially from "Our incident response plan was last tested on [specific date] with [specific outcomes]." Ask for dates, test results, and revision histories.

Red Flag: Response time is unusually fast for complex questionnaires. A thorough response to a 200-question assessment requires input from multiple teams and review cycles. Turnaround under 48 hours suggests templated answers without current validation.

Red Flag: Refusal to provide supporting documentation. Auditors' executive summaries, penetration test result summaries (redacted), and policy excerpts are reasonable requests. Vendors who treat all documentation requests as unreasonable may lack the artifacts to support their claims.

Contractual Protections That Matter

Verification informs negotiation. Based on your findings, include specific contractual terms:

  • Breach Notification Timeline: 72 hours maximum from discovery to notification. Define what constitutes "discovery" to avoid disputes over when the clock starts.
  • Right to Audit: Annual audit rights with reasonable notice periods. Specify whether audits are document-based, interview-based, or include technical testing.
  • Security Requirements Baseline: Reference specific standards (NIST CSF, CIS Controls) rather than vague "industry best practices" language that's difficult to enforce.
  • Subprocessor Approval: Require written approval before adding new subprocessors that will handle your data. Include right to object based on security assessment.
  • Termination Rights: Material security failures should trigger termination rights without penalty. Define materiality specifically rather than leaving it open to interpretation.

Making Verification Sustainable

Initial verification is necessary but insufficient. Security postures change continuously. The vendor who passed your assessment in January may have degraded by June due to staffing changes, infrastructure migrations, or budget cuts.

Sustainable third-party security verification combines periodic re-assessment with continuous monitoring of publicly observable indicators. Automated monitoring catches degradation between formal reviews and provides early warning before issues become incidents.
