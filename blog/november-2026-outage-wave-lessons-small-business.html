Five Outages in Five Days: What the November 2026 Cloud Meltdown Means for Small Business

November 25, 2026 · 10 min read

Between November 12 and November 16, Microsoft 365, the FAA's air traffic control network, AWS us-east-1, JPMorgan Chase, and United Airlines each suffered a major outage. Five organizations. Five different causes. Zero cyberattacks, despite what you may have seen on social media.

The timing made people nervous. Nine disruptions in six days, including Apple and the NSA, felt coordinated. It wasn't. Each incident had its own root cause or none published yet. The shared lesson is more mundane and more useful: when your business depends on a single widely used component, a fault in that component becomes your fault whether you caused it or not.

What Actually Broke

Microsoft 365 (Nov 12-13, ~24 hours). A latent software defect from a July 2026 update to network gateway servers sat undetected for months. Teams had failed over to the Exchange Online gateway about 15 hours earlier, adding load. Almost 14% of gateway capacity was already offline. The defect triggered lock contention that exhausted CPU and memory, and over 80% of gateway servers became unresponsive. Rerouted traffic hit other gateways, which failed the same way. Exchange Online couldn't fail over for scale reasons, restarts didn't hold, and rollback wasn't possible because the change was months old. Engineers deployed an update reverting the defective code path.

FAA Ground Stop (Nov 13, ~4.5 hours). Excess connection traffic, roughly 1,000 attempts every four to five seconds, traced to two sites: a radio communications site in El Dorado, Arkansas with a newly installed router during a copper-to-fiber replacement, and a radar site in Amarillo, Texas. About 3,500 flights were airborne when departures stopped. L3Harris blocked the two source locations and the network stabilized. The FAA disabled the same functionality on 60+ other Juniper routers and paused installations. One of only two nationwide ground stops since September 11, 2001.

AWS us-east-1 (Nov 15, ~3 hours). Authentication errors across 11 services in N. Virginia, including IAM Identity Center sign-in and a subset of Network Load Balancers. AWS hasn't published a root cause. Their updates don't mention a cyberattack.

JPMorgan Chase (Nov 16, duration unspecified). A software bug in an on-premises hardware system. Customers at other banks saw delays on transactions with Chase, including Zelle payments. Chase said it was "not cyber-related."

United Airlines (Nov 16, ~90 minutes). A technology issue affecting departures. The pilots' union said the weight-and-balance system was involved. No impact on flights in progress. United said there was "no indication" of a cyber issue.

The pattern: Software defects, maintenance changes, and capacity planning failures. Not attacks. Not sabotage. The kind of boring infrastructure problems that don't make headlines until they happen to you.

Why This Matters If You're Not Enterprise

You probably don't run air traffic control or process millions of bank transactions. But you likely depend on at least one of these platforms. Microsoft 365 handles email for most small businesses. AWS hosts a significant chunk of the internet's backend services. When they go down, your operations stop regardless of how well you've secured your own systems.

The November wave showed three failure modes that apply directly to smaller organizations.

Latent defects surface under stress. Microsoft's bug existed since July. It only broke things when Teams added unexpected load to a gateway that was already running degraded. Your stack probably has similar time bombs: configurations that work fine at current scale but fail when traffic spikes, dependencies that haven't been updated because they're working, capacity buffers that looked adequate last quarter.

Failover isn't automatic just because you pay for redundancy. Exchange Online couldn't fail over to alternative infrastructure "for scale reasons." Teams and SharePoint recovered faster because they could switch backends. Having redundant components doesn't help if the failover mechanism itself depends on the thing that's broken. Test your failover. Actually test it. On a schedule.

Outages create secondary security risks. During the Microsoft 365 outage, threat actors registered lookalike domains and sent "mailbox resync" phishing lures to frustrated employees. Administrators under pressure to restore service disabled MFA, relaxed Conditional Access policies, and forwarded sensitive documents to personal email accounts. These gaps outlasted the outage. When your team is scrambling, security controls get loosened. Plan for that.

What to Check Before the Next One

You can't prevent Microsoft or AWS from having a bad day. You can reduce how badly their bad day affects you.

  • Map your single points of failure. Which services would stop your business if they went down for 24 hours? Email, payment processing, customer database, deployment pipeline? For each one, ask: do we have a backup path that doesn't share the same underlying infrastructure?
  • Keep break-glass access separate. If your identity provider goes down, how do you authenticate? Maintain at least one admin account with credentials stored outside the primary system. Test that it works quarterly.
  • Run in more than one location with live traffic. Multi-region only helps if both regions are serving users. Cold standby takes too long to warm up during an incident. If you can't afford multi-region, at minimum keep recent backups tested and restorable within your recovery time objective.
  • Document your outage playbook before you need it. Who gets notified? What services get triaged first? Where do status updates go? Writing this during an incident guarantees mistakes. Write it now.
  • Audit security controls after every major disruption. Did anyone disable MFA to restore access? Were temporary permissions revoked after the incident? Did any sensitive data get forwarded to unmonitored channels? Treat post-outage review as a security audit, not just a root cause analysis.

The Bigger Question

TechRepublic put it plainly: keeping individual systems online matters, but knowing what happens when they go offline matters just as much. The November 2026 wave wasn't a coordinated attack or a systemic collapse. It was five independent reminders that complex systems fail in complex ways, and the organizations that recover fastest are the ones that planned for failure before it happened.

You don't need enterprise budgets to plan for outages. You need honest answers about where your dependencies live, tested recovery procedures, and the discipline to maintain security controls even when everything is on fire. The next wave won't announce itself. Be ready anyway.
